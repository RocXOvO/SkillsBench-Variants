\subsection{Generalization under Task Variations}
\label{sec:variant-transfer}

We evaluate source-task skills under changes to input representation (P1), task-instance structure (P2), and coupled task requirements (P3). The final record covers 222 task variants and 886 condition-level results across no skill and the three methods, including completed recovery evaluations. For each method, Table~\ref{tab:variant-transfer} summarizes the recorded reward difference from a no-skill execution of the same variant.

\paragraph{SkillEvolver.} Across 222 variants, mean recorded reward increases from 41.87 to 53.81, a mean within-variant gain of 11.94 points, with 47 improvements and 20 regressions. Gains remain positive under all three types of change: 11.85 points on P1, 14.56 on P2, and 9.72 on P3. The strongest gain occurs when the task instance changes, consistent with learned procedures adapting beyond the original inputs.

\paragraph{SPARK.} Across 221 variants, mean recorded reward increases from 41.61 to 42.25, a mean within-variant gain of 0.65 points, with 32 improvements and 37 regressions. Gains are 2.00 points on P1 and 4.53 on P3, while P2 produces a 5.23-point decline.

\subsection{Skill Reuse under Task Variation}
\label{sec:skill-reuse}

SkillEvolver's positive gains across P1--P3 indicate that the utility of its frozen skills extends beyond the source instances. The weaker or negative variant gains of SPARK and CoEvoSkills show that improvement on the original task and usefulness after task changes are distinct properties. This makes the content preserved during reuse central to interpreting the results.

Two complementary forms of content emerge from the inspected skill files and execution traces: diagnostic rules for choosing and revising a procedure, and constraints for checking the resulting output. In the examined SkillEvolver cases, these rules remain explicit while input handling and solution construction adapt to the variant. Corresponding failure traces reveal that reusing a procedure can preserve much of the workflow while missing a decision or constraint needed for correctness. These observations suggest that reusable decision rules, including when they apply and how their outputs should be checked, are a useful unit of analysis for skill transfer.

Within the lifecycle audit, variant performance characterizes the scope of observed utility. The subsequent mechanism studies examine persistence under fresh deployment (Section~\ref{sec:retained-skill-effects}) and the contributions of feedback, revision, selection, skill content, and evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:attributing-skill-gains}).
